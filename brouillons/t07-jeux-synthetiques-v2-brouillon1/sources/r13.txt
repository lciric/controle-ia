=== D 1

TITLE: Out-of-context implication deduction as a function of bridging subjects
PROPOSAL: We build a synthetic corpus of triples where each subject has a fact relation r1 with answer b and, for a fraction of subjects, the implied relation r2 with answer c under a fixed rule from b to c. We train a two-layer transformer with learned embeddings from scratch on the corpus and test whether subjects seen only in the fact receive the correct implication, sweeping the fraction of bridging subjects from 0 to 0.5 and the number of rules from 1 to 50.
CONTROLS: A one-layer attention-only model, a bag-of-words model that cannot bind subject and relation, a corpus where the rule is broken by random c assignments, and a baseline that predicts the most frequent c. We report implication accuracy on held-out subjects and the rate of answers taken from the wrong rule, over three seeds.
COST: 40
PREDICTION: Implication accuracy should rise sharply once a small fraction of bridging subjects is present and then saturate, with the one-layer model staying near chance. If the broken-rule corpus gives the same accuracy, the model is exploiting answer frequency rather than deducing.

TITLE: One associative weight behind generalisation and hallucination after knowledge injection
PROPOSAL: We train a two-layer transformer on synthetic triples with a rule linking relation r1 answers to relation r2 answers, inject a fresh batch of facts about unseen subjects by fine-tuning, and measure two quantities on the same subjects: correct deduction of the implied r2 answer, and hallucination, defined as a confident r2 answer for subjects whose r1 answer lies outside the rule, or a confident answer under an unrelated relation r3. We then test whether the two track each other through training and across the injection learning rate.
CONTROLS: Fine-tuning with the implication-bearing subjects removed from pretraining, a model with the attention head that links r1 to r2 ablated, injection by in-context presentation instead of fine-tuning, and a shuffled-rule corpus. We record both rates every hundred steps over three seeds.
COST: 60
PREDICTION: Deduction accuracy and hallucination rate should rise together and the head ablation should lower both at once. If hallucination appears without deduction under the shuffled rule, the two are not one mechanism.
=== D 3
ACTION: ADD
TITLE: Training dynamics of implication transfer in a simplified transformer
PROPOSAL: We analyse gradient flow for a one-layer transformer with a single attention head followed by a two-layer MLP, trained with cross-entropy on synthetic triples under a rule from r1 answers to r2 answers, with orthogonal subject embeddings. We aim to prove that the MLP learns a map from the b answer direction to the c answer direction that is shared across subjects, and that this map transfers to a subject trained only on its r1 fact because the attention head writes the subject's b direction into the residual stream at the r2 query. We derive the time at which transfer appears as a function of the number of bridging subjects and the embedding dimension.
CONTROLS: Numerical simulation of the same model at dimensions 64, 256 and 1024 to check the predicted transfer time, a model without the MLP that should fail to transfer, and a 125M model trained on the same corpus to see whether the derived scaling holds beyond the simplified setting.
COST: 24
PREDICTION: Transfer time should scale inversely with the number of bridging subjects and the simulation should match the theory within a constant factor. The theory could fail if the attention head learns a subject-specific rather than a relation-level pattern, which the 125M model would reveal.

TITLE: Why implications transfer but reversals do not
PROPOSAL: We train the same two-layer transformer on two synthetic corpora, one where a rule links r1 answers to r2 answers for the same subject and one where the test requires answering the reversed relation from answer to subject, and we trace the attention and MLP pathways to explain why the first generalises for new subjects and the second does not. We look for whether the forward rule is stored as an answer-to-answer map in the MLP, which a reversal cannot exploit because the subject is never written into the answer position.
CONTROLS: A corpus where reversed sentences are included for half the subjects, a model with tied input and output embeddings, a shuffled-rule corpus, and the same probes on a 125M model trained on both corpora, with three seeds.
COST: 50
PREDICTION: Implication transfer should appear well before reversal transfer, which should stay at chance unless reversed examples are present. If tied embeddings restore reversal, the asymmetry lies in the embedding geometry rather than in the training dynamics.
