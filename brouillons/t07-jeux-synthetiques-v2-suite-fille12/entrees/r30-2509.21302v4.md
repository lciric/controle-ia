## Problem statement (the only description of the task you may use)

Learning-based 3D reconstruction has recently shown unprecedented capabilities in recovering dense geometry and camera trajectories directly from image sequences. Traditional approaches are grounded in geometric priors and optimization, but their reliance on handcrafted design choices and iterative solvers often leads to limited scalability and reduced robustness in complex scenes. Large-scale deep models have instead shifted the paradigm toward data-driven frameworks that generalize remarkably well across diverse environments. A milestone in this evolution is the Visual Geometry Grounded Transformer (VGGT), a 1.2-billion-parameter transformer that unifies multiple 3D tasks, including dense depth estimation, point map regression, camera pose prediction and point tracking, within a single forward pass, and consistently surpasses task-specialized counterparts. VGGT tokenizes each input frame with a pretrained vision backbone and adds to every frame a small set of learned special tokens (a camera token and register tokens) that aggregate global context and cross-view geometry before all tokens are processed by the transformer backbone.

Despite this success, the billion-scale parameterization of VGGT incurs prohibitive computational and memory costs, which severely restrict its deployment in real-world, resource-constrained scenarios. Model quantization, which converts the weights and activations of a model from high-precision floating point to low-precision integers, is an effective way to compress and accelerate models. Among quantization paradigms, post-training quantization (PTQ) is particularly attractive for large models: unlike quantization-aware training, which requires substantial data and massive training resources, PTQ keeps the pretrained weights fixed and only determines the quantization parameters from a small calibration set. PTQ has been widely validated on large language models (for example GPTQ and SmoothQuant) and on 2D vision models (for example PTQ4ViT and PTQ4DiT), but the quantization of billion-scale 3D reconstruction transformers such as VGGT remains largely unexplored, and it is not known whether techniques developed for language or 2D vision models carry over to them.

Two model-specific properties make the quantization of VGGT particularly challenging. The first is the presence of data-independent special tokens. Unlike regular image tokens, which are encoded from the input images, the camera and register tokens are pretrained and injected alongside the image tokens. This data-independent property can make activation distributions deviate from typical patterns, amplifying heavy tails and producing extreme variance across channels and tokens; when a few very large values occupy most of the quantization range, standard quantization suffers substantial information loss. The second is the inherent semantic complexity of 3D data. Each input sequence consists of several non-identical and complex views, so the underlying semantic space is both high-dimensional and highly redundant. Calibration should capture the major part of the expected data distribution, but if the few calibration samples are rare outliers or lack diversity, the estimated quantization ranges become biased and fail to generalize, degrading performance on unseen scenes. Sample diversity and representativeness are therefore far more critical than in 2D vision tasks.

The problem is thus how to perform accurate post-training quantization of a billion-scale multi-view 3D reconstruction transformer. How can its weights and activations be quantized to low bit-widths while keeping camera pose and geometry accuracy close to that of the full-precision model and delivering real memory savings and speed-ups on hardware? How should the heavy-tailed, high-variance activation statistics associated with data-independent special tokens be handled without adding meaningful inference cost? And how should a small calibration set be drawn from complex multi-view 3D data so that the resulting quantization ranges are representative, robust to outlier samples and stable across sample selections, and generalize to unseen scenes?
