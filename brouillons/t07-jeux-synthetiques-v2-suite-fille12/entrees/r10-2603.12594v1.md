## Problem statement (the only description of the task you may use)

Self-supervised contrastive learning has become a cornerstone paradigm for representation learning, enabling models to extract rich semantic features without explicit labels. At its core, contrastive learning constructs a latent space in which semantically similar samples (different augmented views of the same instance) converge while dissimilar samples diverge, and it is frequently guided by information-theoretic objectives such as maximizing the mutual information between views. Its empirical success has motivated extensive theoretical work: contrastive objectives have been decomposed into alignment and uniformity principles, related to mutual information maximization and noise contrastive estimation, and connected to spectral clustering on a data graph. Despite these advances, a fundamental tension persists between theoretical understanding and practical implementation. Existing analyses offer limited insight into the dynamic information landscape that unfolds during the learning process, in particular into how much new information the views presented to the model actually contribute for each individual instance.

In practice, current contrastive frameworks such as SimCLR, MoCo and their variants rely heavily on static, human-engineered augmentations and on large-batch sampling to enforce invariance and representational diversity. These choices impose rigid constraints on the learning dynamics: augmentations must balance semantic preservation against transformational complexity, while batch scaling faces unavoidable hardware limitations. Despite substantial engineering efforts to refine augmentation strategies, these methods fundamentally lack a principled mechanism for adaptively expanding the representational capacity of each instance while maintaining semantic coherence.

This leaves several open questions. How can the information gained through the views of an instance be characterized in a principled, information-theoretic way, so that augmentation design is guided by more than heuristics? Can the diversity of views be expanded adaptively and in a controlled manner, rather than only through fixed hand-crafted augmentation pipelines or ever larger batches, without sacrificing the semantic consistency that views of the same instance must share? And how should the trade-off between semantic invariance and representational expressivity be managed so that contrastive learning remains effective in small-batch, hardware-constrained settings?
