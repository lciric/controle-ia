## Problem statement (the only description of the task you may use)

Large language models (LLMs) are prone to hallucination: they produce responses that are factually incorrect, nonsensical, or not grounded in the input or available data, while still appearing fluent and plausible. A commonly used mitigation is Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), in which the model is given relevant information retrieved from external knowledge bases. In many settings, such as news summarization, extracting information from a provided structured file, or answering questions that require information emerging after the model's release date, retrieval is necessary because the model cannot rely solely on its internal knowledge to complete the task. Yet even when the retrieved documents are relevant, correct and sufficient, RAG systems still hallucinate, producing statements that are unsupported by, or contradict, the retrieved information.

Recent work has shown that such failures often arise from conflicts between an LLM's internal (parametric) knowledge and the retrieved external context: models tend to over-rely on their internal knowledge regardless of its correctness, which undermines factual reliability. This motivates a principled intuition. When a task requires external knowledge and the retriever supplies sufficient information, the model should use that context and ground its reasoning in it; a response that relies disproportionately on the model's internal knowledge, without a commensurate use of the external context, should therefore be considered less reliable. Stated more formally, for an LLM that receives a query q together with relevant and sufficient retrieved documents d and generates a response a, one can posit a signal of external context utilization E(a | q, d) and a signal of internal knowledge utilization I(a | q, d), and conjecture that a is more likely to be a hallucination when I is much larger than E.

Several recent approaches have tried to quantify these two signals to detect hallucinations in RAG. For instance, one method, ReDeEP, measures external context utilization through the cosine similarity between the generated token and the context tokens that receive high attention weights from certain attention heads, and internal knowledge utilization through the Jensen-Shannon divergence between hidden states before and after the feed-forward layer of certain transformer layers; a follow-up work combines this idea with semantic entropy probes. Although these approaches can detect hallucinations, they have two major limitations. First, they rely on mechanistic-interpretability heuristics: specific attention heads and transformer layers must be selected to compute the scores, a non-trivial selection that requires extensive hyperparameter tuning and is dataset- and model-specific, which limits generalization across datasets and models. Second, although they show that their scores correlate with hallucination, they do not validate whether the scores truly reflect the utilization of external context and internal knowledge. In addition, hallucination detectors of this kind are usually developed under strong assumptions, namely that the retrieved documents are correct, sufficient and relevant, and that the LLM used to compute the detection signals is the same LLM that generated the response; these assumptions can strongly affect performance and limit usability, for instance to responses generated by open-source models only.

The core question is therefore how to quantify an LLM's utilization of external context and of internal knowledge during generation in a way that does not hinge on selecting particular attention heads or layers, generalizes across models and datasets, can be shown to actually capture the intended notion of utilization, and yields an effective and practical detector of hallucinations in RAG systems.
